Griasa

One macOS menu-bar app that types what you say into any app, and turns the meetings you record into the things you owe people.

Native Swift · macOS 14+ GPL-3.0 Source on GitHub

Griasa began as dictation and kept going, because dictation on its own solves half a problem: you can say the words, and then you still have to remember what you agreed to. So the same app records the call, extracts who promised what, keeps a page per colleague, and hands you a brief before the next meeting with them.

Speech recognition runs on the Mac itself — Apple's recogniser or a local whisper.cpp model. Only the optional text-cleanup step can reach a language model, and only if you turn it on.

What it does, in one place

  • Hold a key and talk. The words appear in whatever app has focus — Telegram, Slack, a terminal, a browser field — while you are still speaking.
  • Record a meeting from system audio and the microphone together, and get a transcript with speaker names and your own live notes woven in.
  • Commitments are pulled out of that transcript automatically — who took what on and by when — split into what you promised and what you are waiting on. One click sends any of them to Apple Reminders.
  • A page per person, built from the meeting roster with no setup: every meeting with them, their open promises, and an on-demand dossier written from the transcripts they appear in.
  • A brief before the call. Minutes before a calendar event, what you last discussed, what each of you promised, and a button that opens the Zoom or Meet link and starts recording in one go.
  • Typed abbreviations that expand in place, anywhere, with live values rather than static text.
  • Capture from any window — select text or drag a rectangle over the screen, and turn it into a reminder, plain text, or a drafted reply that read the visible conversation.
  • Projects that everything files itself into, and which you can then ask questions about across your own history.

None of these is a separate tool bolted on. They share one history, one project model and one AI configuration, which is the part that is actually hard to retrofit later.

What the standard AI apps don't do

  • It types into other people's apps, not into its own box. Most assistants give you a text area and a copy button. Griasa puts the words where your cursor already is, as you speak, and never overwrites what you typed yourself.
  • You can ask a model mid-sentence, in someone else's window. Type ;ai what's the capital of Armenia ;; inside a chat composer and the question is replaced in place by the answer. No window switch, no paste.
  • Abbreviations expand to real data. {slots:3} becomes three genuinely free slots read from your calendar; {commitments} becomes your actually-open promises. Not a template you fill in afterwards.
  • The meeting turns into obligations, not just a summary. "I'll send it by Friday" becomes a dated item assigned to the person who said it, deduplicated against what is already open, exportable as tasks that Todoist, Things and Linear split into individual rows.
  • It uses the AI subscription you already pay for. If the claude or codex CLI is installed and logged in, Griasa answers through it — no API key, no second subscription. Or point it at Ollama and the whole thing runs with nothing leaving the Mac.

Why this is harder than it looks

Dictation looks like a small app: capture audio, call a recogniser, insert the text. Every one of those three steps hid a problem that only appears once real people use it.

Typing live means never being allowed to take a word back

A recogniser revises its guess constantly — "тестирую" becomes "тестируй" becomes "тестирую" again. Retype the line each time and you delete whatever the user typed in between. The fix was a policy, not a patch: commit only the words two successive hypotheses agree on, append and never rewrite, and when they disagree, stall rather than invent. The naive version passed a casual test and ate a sentence the first time someone typed during a pause.

The order of results is not guaranteed

Dispatching each partial result onto the main actor as its own task looks correct and is not: nothing promises they arrive in the order they were sent. Two transposed partials and the app's model of the text field no longer describes the text field — and every edit after that is computed against a fiction. Ordering had to be carried explicitly, in a queue under a lock.

The accessibility APIs lie

The clean way to replace text in another app is the accessibility API. Slack declares its composer writable and then silently ignores the write. Telegram declares it read-only, and reports emoji back as U+FFFC. Measured across three apps before designing anything — which is why synthetic keystrokes are the primary path and the tidy API is only an optimisation for apps that tell the truth.

Local speech models get stuck in loops

One spoken sentence came back seven times in a row. That is a known failure mode of greedy decoding, and no amount of prompt tuning fixes it. It took an entropy threshold, a cap on carried context, and a collapser that can tell human emphasis — "no, no, no" — from a decoder going in circles.

Shipping it is its own engineering problem

An unsigned disk image passes Apple's notarisation, accepts a stapled ticket, and reports success at every step — then Gatekeeper refuses it on the customer's Mac, because notarisation is not a signature. Failures that only appear after both round trips to Apple are the expensive kind, so the release script now verifies the way a stranger's Mac will, and refuses to call a build shippable when it isn't.

Also, for the record: macOS ties privacy permissions to an app's signature, so an unstable signing identity silently resets them on every rebuild; and NSLog reaches nowhere at all from an app launched by the Finder, which means the error paths were invisible exactly when they mattered.

Try it, read it, or support it

The build is free and signed — download it and it opens with a double-click. The source is all here too, under GPL-3.0, and ./build.sh produces the same app if you would rather compile it yourself. There is no licence check; under this licence there could not be a meaningful one.

$29 makes my day — but any amount does, and none is fine too. Use it for a week before you decide.
